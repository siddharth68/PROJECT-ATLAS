Presentation — Problem 2
8 slides. 8 minutes. Every member speaks.
Submitted as PDF or PPTX before your session, not at it. Judges skim it while you set up; a deck arriving during the session is not read.
Why we ask for this
Automated scoring tells us whether your system works. It cannot tell us whether you understand what you built, whether the whole team contributed, or whether you know where your own weaknesses are. Eight minutes tells us all three.

Final presentation — all three problems
12 slides. 10 minutes. Every member speaks.
Submitted as PDF or PPTX before the final session. This is the deck that decides the event, together with your automated scores.
Who is in the room
Two technical judges and one clinical judge who has never seen your code and does not want to. Build the deck so both get what they need:
the technical judges want the architecture, the trade-offs and the honesty
the clinical judge wants to know whether they would trust this on a real study
If your deck only speaks to one of them, you lose half the room.

What loses teams the room
Seen every year:
Spending four slides on architecture and none on a real patient. The clinical judge stops listening at slide three.
“Our system achieved 94% accuracy.” Accuracy of what, against what? Give the number that matters: which signals you caught, and when.
Claiming no limitations. Every system has them. Naming yours first is worth more than hoping nobody asks.
One person presenting everything. It reads as a team where one person did everything — and if that is true, we cannot offer the extension track.
Running over. It costs delivery points and it eats your own Q&A time.
No demo video. Then the laptop fails. It happens most years.
